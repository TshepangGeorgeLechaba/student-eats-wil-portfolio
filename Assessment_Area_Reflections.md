# Student Eats — Assessment Area Reflections

**Module:** PRP37XS — Project Presentation III  
**Institution:** Cape Peninsula University of Technology (CPUT)  
**Project:** Student Eats  
**Portfolio component:** Entrepreneurial Work-Integrated Learning (eWIL)

## 1. Agile Team Charter

### Evidence

The Agile Team Charter documents the Student Eats project vision, team roles, working agreements, communication methods, Agile approach, risks, constraints and definition of done.

### Reflection

The team charter helped establish a shared understanding of the Student Eats project and the way the team intended to work together. Defining roles and working agreements is important because team members need to understand their responsibilities and coordinate their contributions towards a common goal.

This artefact also demonstrates the importance of planning before implementation. Identifying risks and constraints can help a team anticipate difficulties and agree on realistic expectations. The charter provides a reference point for reviewing whether the team's activities align with its intended approach.

To improve this process in future projects, I would review the charter regularly as the project develops and document any agreed changes to roles, responsibilities or working arrangements. The charter should be used as an active project-management document rather than treated only as an initial planning exercise.

## 2. Agile User Stories and Tasks

### Evidence

The Agile User Stories and Backlogs document records user-story planning for the Student Eats marketplace, including proposed acceptance criteria and supporting sprint-planning information. The Trello export provides additional evidence of how project tasks and work items were organised.

### Reflection

User stories help translate a project's purpose into requirements from the perspective of its intended users. For Student Eats, considering both students looking for affordable meals and student food vendors helps keep planning connected to the needs of the people the marketplace is intended to serve.

Acceptance criteria are also important because they clarify what a feature should achieve and provide a basis for checking whether the requirement has been met. Breaking larger requirements into manageable tasks can improve clarity and help team members coordinate their work.

This process has shown me the value of making requirements specific and understandable before development begins. In future work, I would ensure that each story has clear acceptance criteria, an agreed priority and an identified person responsible for the work. I would also distinguish clearly between proposed requirements and features that have been implemented and tested.

## 3. Product Backlog

### Evidence

The Agile User Stories and Backlogs document describes the product backlog and planned Student Eats functionality. The Trello export provides supporting evidence of the project's work organisation and prioritisation activities.

### Reflection

The product backlog provides a structured list of requirements and potential improvements for the Student Eats project. It helps the team organise work around the project's objectives instead of attempting to develop every possible feature at once.

For a student food marketplace, features such as discovering meals, viewing food information and placing orders relate to the intended customer experience. However, a feature's presence in the backlog does not by itself prove that the feature has been completed.

I have learned that backlog management requires ongoing review. Priorities may change as the team develops a better understanding of user needs, technical constraints and available time. In future projects, I would review backlog priorities regularly, keep descriptions clear and record status changes so that the planned scope remains realistic and transparent.

## 4. Sprint Backlog

### Evidence

The project documentation and Trello export record sprint planning across activities such as research and planning, user stories, UI/UX, registration, login, marketplace development, cart and ordering, testing, and documentation.

### Reflection

The sprint backlog breaks project work into smaller activities that can be planned and reviewed over a defined period. Organising Student Eats work into stages provides a structure for coordinating design, development, testing and documentation.

The sprint sequence also highlights the relationship between activities. For example, planning and interface design help clarify requirements before related development and testing tasks are undertaken. At the same time, sprint plans must remain flexible enough to accommodate unresolved tasks and technical difficulties.

This process has helped me understand why progress tracking and realistic planning matter in Agile project coordination. In future sprints, I would ensure that each sprint has a clear goal, that selected tasks are achievable within the available time, and that incomplete work is recorded honestly and carried forward when appropriate. Sprint completion should be supported by evidence rather than assumed from the original plan.

## 5. Agile Logbook

### Evidence

The Agile logbooks record project activities and progress across the documented dates. They are intended to support reflection on work completed, upcoming activities, impediments, risks and problems encountered during the project.

### Reflection

The logbook is important because it provides a record of the project's progress over time. It can help connect daily activities to the wider project objectives and provide evidence of participation, planning and problem-solving.

Reflecting on work across different dates can also reveal where progress was made, where tasks remained incomplete and where further action was needed. This information supports accountability and can help a team make better decisions during subsequent work periods.

I recognise that logbook entries need to be accurate, specific and consistent with the original records. In future projects, I would update the logbook regularly, record completed activities and outstanding work separately, and identify impediments and next steps clearly. I would also check that the dates and descriptions correspond to the work actually undertaken.

## Overall Reflection

The five assessment areas are connected. The team charter establishes the intended way of working, user stories describe requirements, the product backlog organises those requirements, the sprint backlog supports short-term planning, and the logbook records project activities and progress.

Together, these artefacts demonstrate the role of planning, collaboration, documentation and reflection in an Agile project. The Student Eats portfolio also provides an opportunity to consider the needs of student customers and vendors when making design and development decisions.

A key lesson for me is that project documentation should reflect actual work, not only intentions. Keeping records accurate, updating plans when circumstances change and supporting claims with evidence are practices I would carry into future multimedia and technology projects.
